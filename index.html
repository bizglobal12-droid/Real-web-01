Module 1: AI Fundamentals

History of AI

Artificial Intelligence began as a concept in the mid-20th century, notably with Alan Turing's proposition of machine intelligence. The field officially started at the Dartmouth Conference in 1956. It has experienced periods of high optimism followed by "AI winters" (reduced funding/interest) before culminating in the current boom driven by vast data and computing power.

Machine Learning (ML)

A subset of AI where systems learn from data to identify patterns and make decisions with minimal human intervention. Instead of explicitly programming rules, models are trained on datasets.

Natural Language Processing (NLP)

Enables computers to understand, interpret, and generate human language. It bridges human communication and computer understanding.

Deep Learning

A subset of ML based on artificial neural networks with multiple layers (hence "deep"). It excels at finding complex patterns in massive amounts of unstructured data.

Computer Vision

A field of AI that enables computers to derive meaningful information from digital images, videos, and other visual inputs, and take actions based on that information.

Module 2: AI Fluency & LLMs

Large Language Models (LLMs)

LLMs are massive deep learning models pre-trained on vast amounts of text data. They use transformer architectures to predict the next word in a sequence, enabling them to generate human-like text, translate languages, write code, and answer complex questions.

Transformers Parameters Tokens

Developing AI Fluency

AI fluency is the ability to understand how AI systems work, their capabilities, limitations, and the ethical implications of using them.

  • Understanding Capabilities Knowing what tasks AI excels at (summarization, ideation) vs. what it struggles with (factual verification).
  • Recognizing Hallucinations The ability to identify when an LLM generates false or nonsensical information presented confidently.
  • Ethical Awareness Understanding bias in training data, privacy concerns, and responsible deployment.
  • Critical Evaluation Not taking outputs at face value and employing human-in-the-loop verification.

Module 3: Data Pipeline

Datasets

The foundational fuel for AI models. A dataset is a structured collection of data. In AI, quality trumps quantity. Curating diverse, unbiased, and clean datasets is crucial for training robust models.

Data Annotation

The process of adding metadata (tags/labels) to raw data (text, images, audio) so machines can understand it.

  • Bounding boxes (CV)
  • Named Entity Recognition (NLP)
  • Sentiment tagging
  • RLHF preference ranking

Data Transcribing

Converting spoken language (audio/video) into written text. This is a critical step for creating datasets for Speech-to-Text models or enabling LLMs to process multimedia content. Accuracy and handling of accents/noise are key challenges.

Module 4: Prompt Engineering & Eval

Prompt Engineering

The art and science of communicating effectively with AI models to elicit desired responses. It involves crafting inputs structurally to guide the model.

Common Mistakes
  • Being too vague or open-ended.
  • Lacking context or role definition.
  • Asking for multiple complex tasks in one go.
  • Not specifying desired output format.
AI Evaluation Focus Areas
Factuality Relevance Safety/Toxicity Tone/Persona
BAD PROMPT

"Write about marketing."

Why: Too broad, no target audience, no length specified, no format.

GOOD PROMPT

"Act as a senior digital marketer. Write a 300-word introduction for a blog post aimed at small business owners explaining the benefits of social media advertising. Use an encouraging tone."

Why: Defines role, length, target audience, topic, and tone.
